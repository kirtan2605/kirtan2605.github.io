Finding radiation signatures in MATS satellite images

How can an atmospheric image reveal a particle strike on its detector? This team study built an image-processing pipeline to isolate candidate single-event upsets in MATS CCD data, mapped when and where they appeared, and compared the patterns with a modeled radiation environment.

The question

When is a bright pixel a radiation event?

MATS observes airglow and noctilucent clouds to study the middle atmosphere. Its CCD also operates in low Earth orbit, where energetic particles can leave isolated bright pixels or short tracks in an image. Those marks can be confused with the scene, ordinary detector noise, or persistent damaged pixels.

The task was to turn timestamped images into candidate event detections, then ask whether their timing, location, and shape made physical sense. The report calls these detections single-event upsets (SEUs); on this page, “candidate” matters because a bright pixel alone is not proof of a particular particle or electronics fault.

02 · Detect brief changes

Binned images reveal where candidate events cluster

Most of each binned frame is the atmospheric scene. Removing what persists across neighboring frames, then what resembles its local neighborhood, leaves short-lived bright peaks to count.

Why a threshold near 600? The report derives a binned-image floor from assumed dark-signal variation: 180 electrons per pixel per second × 80 pixels per bin × 5 seconds, then converts it to approximately 600 digital counts. This is a model-based cutoff, not a detector-independent constant.

03 · Inspect individual shapes

Full-frame images show pixels and tracks

The full-frame pipeline treated bright isolated pixels and connected tracks separately. Unlike the binned workflow, it did not use median filtering: in these images that step increased apparent detections rather than suppressing noise.

A baseline threshold of 350 digital counts per pixel came from the report's dark-signal and exposure assumptions. The team also tested 280, 420, and 525 counts to see how many features disappeared as the cutoff became stricter. Connected-pixel analysis then identified track-like candidates; energy deposition and linear energy transfer (LET) were estimated from their intensity and length under a simplified detector calibration.

05 · Limits and next steps

A useful detection framework, not a calibrated particle census

The analysis demonstrates a practical way to find transient radiation-like signatures in MATS imagery and relate their distribution to the orbital environment. Its conclusions depend on fixed noise thresholds, assumed linear conversion from pixel value to deposited energy, and simple rules for classifying track shapes.

Persistent hot pixels and changing detector background can mimic events. The report's heavy-particle candidate counts also vary non-monotonically across thresholds, so they should not be read as verified population estimates. A stronger follow-up would identify damaged pixels, adapt thresholds to each image's noise, and validate classifications against calibrated detector data.
