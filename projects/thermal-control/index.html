Space systems · Thermal engineering

Designing thermal control for the SPOT satellite

How do you stop satellite electronics from overheating in sunlight or freezing when heat escapes to space? This project sized radiators and heaters, tested the design in an orbital thermal model, and refined it until the equipment met its temperature limits in hot, cold, and survival conditions.

The challenge

Balance heat loss and heat gain through every orbit

SPOT’s power supply, onboard data processor, attitude and orbit control system (AOCS), and telemetry equipment each had to remain between −10 °C and 40 °C in nominal operation. The satellite receives heat from the Sun and Earth, produces heat inside its electronics, and radiates heat into deep space. Those inputs change as it moves around Earth and as its surface coating ages.

The design task was to choose where equipment should sit, how much radiator surface each face needs, how powerful its heater should be, and how strongly equipment should conduct heat into the radiator. The goal was to satisfy the temperature limits while avoiding a heater that stays on continuously. The nominal heater duty-cycle target was 60–70% for active heaters.

01 · Define the envelope

Which conditions should the design survive?

The team modeled the most demanding combinations of orbital season and surface age. “Summer” and “winter” name the solstice trajectories used in the simulation; they do not describe weather around the satellite.

SPOT flies in an 830 km sun-synchronous orbit. In normal operation its −Z face points to Earth and its −Y face points along the velocity direction. The Sun, reflected sunlight from Earth (albedo), and Earth’s infrared emission heat its faces differently. The radiator coating absorbs more sunlight as it ages, so the design must work both at beginning of life (BOL) and end of life (EOL).

Nominal attitude

−Z faces Earth while −Y faces the direction of travel. The team checked the hottest and coldest nominal cases over 20 orbits.

Survival attitude

+Z faces the Sun while the spacecraft spins around Z. Equipment dissipates different power, so the final design also had to work in this mode.

02 · Build the model

A first estimate, then an orbital simulation

The team first calculated what each face could reject using a simplified cube. Those estimates became the starting configuration for a detailed thermal network. In the Systema suite, Thermica calculated radiation and orbital heat inputs; Thermisol solved how temperatures changed over time.

  1. 01

    Find the heat entering each face

    Thermica calculated absorbed solar radiation, Earth albedo, and Earth infrared radiation during an orbit for all four environmental cases.

  2. 02

    Match equipment to cooling capacity

    The team compared the heat each face could reject at 0 °C and 20 °C, then placed the highest-dissipation equipment on the strongest cooling face.

  3. 03

    Calculate an initial hardware size

    A heat balance set radiator area at a 20 °C target. A second balance estimated heater power at 0 °C with equipment in standby, when it produces less heat.

  4. 04

    Test the coupled design over time

    A nodal model added walls, insulation, radiators, equipment, and their heat-transfer paths. The solver then calculated temperatures across 20 orbital cycles.

Why place them this way?

At the hot-case 20 °C calculation point, +Z could reject about 247.5 W/m², the most of the four available faces, so it received the 150 W power supply. −Z could reject about 162.1 W/m², the least, and received the 15 W data processor. The ±Y faces were used for AOCS and telemetry.

This arrangement was a starting point for simulation, not proof that the design would pass every case.

Radiator area

A = Pdiss / (εσT4 − fabs)

Heater power

Pheater = Arad(femit − fabs) − Pstandby

In plain terms, only emitted heat minus absorbed external heat is available to cool equipment. A larger radiator can reject more heat, while a heater replaces heat lost when equipment is quiet. In the equations, ε is radiator emissivity, σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, T is radiator temperature, and fabs is absorbed environmental heat flux. The first sizing aimed for 0–20 °C to leave margin inside the actual −10–40 °C operating limits; non-radiator areas were assumed to have MLI.

03 · Iterate the design

The cold case exposed an over-cooled design

The first model used the preliminary radiator and heater sizes with the strongest available equipment-to-radiator connection: 40 W/K thermal joints. It passed the hot case with substantial margin, but the cold case failed.

↳

Diagnosis: the hot case had room to run warmer, while the cold case lost too much heat. The team therefore resized radiators for a 30 °C target instead of 20 °C and retuned heater power to aim for 60–70% on time rather than continuous operation.

First correction

Pass nominal hot and cold

Smaller radiators and individually adjusted heaters kept the updated cold-case temperatures within range. In that intermediate design, telemetry, AOCS, and power-supply heater duty cycles were 60.9%, 66.0%, and 63.0% respectively.

Final correction

Add survival mode

The team then simulated the +Z Sun-facing spin and placed the TTC antenna on +Y. About thirty recorded trials adjusted radiator areas, heater ratings, and equipment-to-radiator conductance until nominal and survival requirements worked together.

Final architecture

What changed between the first and final designs?

Radiator areas were reduced or redistributed. Thermal washers limited heat loss from telemetry and onboard processing, while direct attachment gave AOCS and the power supply stronger heat paths. Heater power is the installed rating; duty cycle describes how long it runs.

05 · Limits and lessons

A balanced preliminary design with clear next steps

The first hot-case pass alone would have hidden a cold-case failure. Running the extremes together, then adding survival mode, revealed how radiator area, heater duty cycle, and equipment-to-radiator conductance interact. Recording each change during approximately thirty survival-mode trials made that trade-off easier to diagnose.

The result is a preliminary simulated design. The network is deliberately simplified and leaves the instrument and solar-panel elements inactive. The report recommends a more detailed model, more nodes, activating those elements, and further thermal analysis and testing before a flight design could be claimed.
