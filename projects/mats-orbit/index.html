Understanding the MATS orbit, contacts, and lines of sight

A satellite's orbit sets when it sees sunlight, when a ground station can talk to it, and which other spacecraft are geometrically visible. This team project propagated MATS orbital data and turned those positions into eclipse, Esrange contact, and Starlink line-of-sight analyses.

The question

What does MATS's changing position make possible?

MATS flies in an approximately 585 km, near-circular sun-synchronous orbit. Its position relative to the Sun, Earth, Esrange ground station, and other satellites changes every minute. A mission planner needs more than a single orbit drawing: they need time histories of lighting, eclipse, communication access, and potential lines of sight.

The study asked four practical questions: How does the orbit evolve? When is MATS eclipsed? How often can Esrange see it? And, in a separate geometric experiment, how many Starlink satellites lie above Earth's limb from MATS?

01 · Build the time history

Propagate near each TLE's epoch, then ask geometric questions

A two-line element set (TLE) describes an orbit at a particular epoch. The project gathered successive TLEs rather than treating one set as accurate indefinitely, then calculated positions and derived quantities across the analysis period.

  1. 01

    Retrieve orbital elements

    Collect time-stamped MATS TLEs and, for the constellation study, Starlink TLEs covering the sample period.

  2. 02

    Propagate positions

    Compute satellite states at regular times, staying mindful that TLE accuracy degrades as propagation moves away from its epoch.

  3. 03

    Test mission geometry

    Use Sun direction and Earth's shadow for illumination; use Esrange coordinates and a 10° elevation mask for ground-station access.

  4. 04

    Compare other spacecraft

    For a January 2023 sample, test whether Earth blocks the line between MATS and each of 439 Starlink spacecraft at 60-second steps.

The Starlink sample size and 60-second resolution come from the project's code repository. “Geometrically visible” means the straight line is not blocked by the assumed Earth boundary; it does not imply that a satellite appears in MATS's camera field of view.

02 · Read the orbit

The Sun angle and orbital elements change over time

The analysis followed the angle between the Sun direction and the orbit's angular-momentum vector, alongside changes in semi-major axis and eccentricity. These trends set the context for seasonal lighting and eclipse behavior.

Why it matters: the orbital plane's orientation to the Sun determines whether the spacecraft enters Earth's shadow and how its lighting geometry evolves. The model turns a moving orbit into dates and durations a mission team can plan around.

03 · Plan light and contact

Eclipses and Esrange passes are brief, countable windows

The eclipse calculation used a simplified spherical-Earth shadow model. Esrange visibility was a separate test: MATS had to rise at least 10° above the local horizon for the KIR-1 antenna.

04 · Extend the geometry

Which Starlink satellites are above Earth's limb?

The second analysis propagated MATS and a sampled Starlink population together. At each time step, it asked whether the line between two spacecraft cleared an assumed Earth boundary, then counted the geometrically visible satellites.

Visibility is not interference. The line-of-sight result does not show whether a Starlink satellite crossed the MATS instrument field of view, was illuminated, or affected any measurement. Those are separate tests left for future work.

05 · Interpretation and limits

Useful mission estimates, bounded by the model

Across the study, TLE propagation turned historical orbital elements into interpretable timelines: lighting and eclipse behavior, short Esrange contact windows, and a broad inter-satellite visibility survey. The analysis also explored whether orbital decay aligned with sunspot activity, but the presentation notes that moving-average slopes were noisy; that comparison is exploratory rather than proof of a causal relationship.

The outputs depend on TLE age, the chosen time step, a spherical-Earth approximation, and visibility cutoffs. A follow-up would use higher-fidelity geometry and explicitly test the MATS field of view before drawing conclusions about imaging interference.
